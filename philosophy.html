Judgement

Imani · Cofounder · 2 min read · 542 words

Modern society is built on trust.

Trust in institutions that govern us. Trust in the solvency of our banks. Trust in our engineering and manufacturing industries to stress test products. Trust that the person on the other side of a contract will hold up their end.

Behind every act of trust is an act of judgement. Someone, somewhere, decided that this bank is sound, this borrower will repay, or that if I order on this platform, this shipment will arrive. Progress - in a firm, an industry, a country - is the accumulation of judgements made well.

Judgement runs on data. You hop on a flight and text “landing in 5 hours”, and don’t even think about anything going wrong, because a century of aviation records has already made the judgement for you. Where information is abundant, trust is effortless.

In emerging markets data is scarce, and what exists is scattered. This makes judgement incredibly expensive. And when judgement is expensive, trust retreats to the people you already know - and economies built only on personal networks stay small. Nowhere does this cost more than in the industries at the center of economic life.

  1. Credit - Every high income country went through a period of profound structural shift known as financial deepening. Scarce credit to SMEs in emerging markets is a massive bottleneck affecting GDP growth. Lenders have poor judgement on borrowers due to deeply asymmetric information between the two parties.
  2. Legal - Land disputes. Contract related project delays. Low jurisdictional trust. In Tanzania we’ve observed many infrastructural projects get delayed due to legal friction, often because teams are drowning in paperwork and struggling to decipher applicable international law to make the right call.
  3. Agriculture - Despite holding 60% of the world’s arable land, Africa imports a majority of its food. While lack of machinery is often a problem, the unpredictability of weather patterns, lack of visibility into soil composition, and an overall deficit of data points prevent farmers from making optimal decisions at the right time - what to plant, when to plant, when to sell.
  4. Trade - Africa’s supply chains are deeply inefficient, with around 40% of goods lost in transit. Poor roads and storage are part of it, but so are fragmentation, low coordination, and red tape. Along every supply chain lie numerous decisions that determine efficiency and ultimately how much value is created.
  5. Security - Governments are investing more than ever in protecting mines, borders, and critical systems - physical and digital. What determines the scale of this investment is the baseline judgement on whether we are safe or not.

We believe the most important thing AI can do in emerging markets is improve judgement. Not automating decisions, but supporting them. Allowing decisions which once took months of paperwork, personal connections, or the need for harsh enforcement mechanisms, to take days and hold up to scrutiny. We started Ezana to build this, for enterprises and governments in Tanzania and across Africa.

But AI only earns a place in these decisions if it can itself be trusted. Privacy and protection of sovereign data is paramount. Every movement of data carries its lineage. Every model action leaves an audit trail. Guardrails and security are designed in from day one.

Better judgement, on infrastructure our customers can trust. That is Ezana.
